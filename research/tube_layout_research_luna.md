# Roanoke Star neon tube segmentation: bounded photo research

Research question: where do the physical neon tube sections appear to break on the Mill Mountain / Roanoke Star, especially the six bright all-white contours arranged in three close pairs?

This is a photographic fabrication aid, not a survey or a wiring diagram. The exact historical tube schedule was not found. The notes below distinguish what is visible from what is a safe modeling assumption.

## Sources and evidentiary weight

* **Best close-up source:** Ben Schumin's night photo set, [The Roanoke Star, Night](https://www.schuminweb.com/photography/roanoke-star-night/). The page itself says that, except for the red ring in that display, each ring is lit by two rows of tubing; it also says that many smaller tubes form each ring and shows close views of a top apex, lateral point, lower point, corners, and underside. The individual full-size image URLs used for the observations are [089 (top apex)](https://files.schuminweb.com/photography/roanoke-star/full-size/089.jpg), [091 (apex and many short pieces)](https://files.schuminweb.com/photography/roanoke-star/full-size/091.jpg), [092 (upper side / apex)](https://files.schuminweb.com/photography/roanoke-star/full-size/092.jpg), [098 (paired white tubes)](https://files.schuminweb.com/photography/roanoke-star/full-size/098.jpg), [100 (outer lateral tip)](https://files.schuminweb.com/photography/roanoke-star/full-size/100.jpg), [101 (lower outer tip)](https://files.schuminweb.com/photography/roanoke-star/full-size/101.jpg), [102 (notch / corner hardware)](https://files.schuminweb.com/photography/roanoke-star/full-size/102.jpg), and [103 (apex / corner hardware)](https://files.schuminweb.com/photography/roanoke-star/full-size/103.jpg).
* **Best all-white contour source:** the City of Roanoke's [13604 aerial image](https://www.roanokeva.gov/ImageRepository/Document?documentID=13604), retained locally as `research/references/city_aerial_original.jpg`. It is nearly frontal enough to establish six dominant bright paths grouped as three close pairs, but the star is too small and overexposed for reliable tube-joint positions.
* **Independent all-white close source:** Ben Schumin's [Roanoke Star in white](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Roanoke_Star_in_white.jpg), dated 22 April 2007. It confirms the white display and paired contours, but the available image still does not resolve every glass joint around the complete star.
* **Useful colored close source:** [Mill Mountain Star Neon Lights](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mill_Mountain_Star_Neon_Lights.JPG), 17 November 2008, 2,592 × 1,944. Its red/white/blue close view makes the small dark breaks and tube supports easiest to see. It is evidence for physical segmentation and mounting, not for which circuits are energized in the all-white display.
* **Daylight / aerial sources:** the local Ben Schumin daylight and 2024 aerial photographs are useful for steel channel depth, rear scaffold, and perspective. They do not expose neon glass-joint locations and should not be used to invent a tube schedule.

## What the close-ups actually show

### Tube count and pair arrangement

The close-ups show two parallel illuminated tubes following a ring/frame in the white and blue rings. This agrees with Schumin's caption that the rings other than the red one use two rows. Together with the City's nearly frontal white aerial, the practical all-white topology is six visible tracks: three structural bands, each represented by a close pair.

This does **not** mean six physical glass tubes. Each visible track is made from many shorter sections. The City's 2,000-foot total and its “sets” wording do not publish the individual piece count or electrical grouping.

### Long straight runs

On the upper arm and outer-point close-ups (images 100 and 101), the long straight portions visibly contain multiple short pieces, with the joints away from the geometric point. A coordinate check on the bright lower row of the outer upper-right horizontal arm in the Wikimedia close-up gives approximate endpoints at x=1545 and x=2285, and clear dark interruptions at x=1736, 1915, and 2092. Those fall at about 0.258, 0.500, and 0.739 of the horizontal x-span, suggesting four visible pieces on this particular long run. This is stronger evidence than a generic two-joint 30%/65% schedule for that arm, although the dark interruptions could include connector/terminal hardware or support occlusion.

The same photograph shows that section lengths are not uniform: the first and last pieces on this arm are shorter than the middle pieces. Other sloping legs read as two or three sections, but their exact positions are less clear.

On the sloping legs in images 091, 092, and 103, the same scale is visible: generally two or three sections per straight leg. The shortest inner-star legs sometimes read as one long section plus a shorter terminal section, but image perspective and glow make an exact count uncertain.

These are approximate proportions measured by eye from the close photographs. They are a useful starting schedule, not a claim about the original glass-shop dimensions.

### Apexes and outer tips

The top apex close-ups (089, 091, 092, 103) show a sharp, fully illuminated point. The obvious dark breaks occur down the sloping legs, typically around the middle portions of the legs, rather than as a visibly open gap at the apex itself. Each parallel row follows the same sharp outline.

The outer lateral point (100) and lower outer point (101) show the same behavior: the two rows reach the point and turn sharply into the adjoining leg. The point region is not a long uninterrupted bend; the straight legs have their own visible section boundaries close to the corner. A miniature should therefore keep the light visually continuous through the point while allowing a short section or joint treatment on each adjoining straight leg.

The photos do not prove whether a given corner uses one custom bent glass piece, two pieces meeting at an electrode/connector, or a hidden overlap behind the aluminum channel. Do not encode a precise “joint exactly at every vertex” claim from these images.

### Shoulders and concave notches

Image 100 shows the upper lateral arm ending in a sharp outer tip, while image 102 shows a concave notch where a ring turns from a horizontal run into a steep leg. The tube rows follow the metal member through these tight corners; the dark marks visible near the turns are consistent with tube ends/supports and are not all individually distinguishable as electrical breaks.

The safe physical reading is: straight glass sections terminate close to the corners, and the corner is a deliberately sharp fabricated transition. It is unsafe to infer exact offsets or a universal corner piece length from the low-angle view.

## Are the two paired runs synchronized?

They do not appear to be synchronized. In the close white/blue pair views (098, 102, and 103), a dark break on one row is visibly displaced along the run from the corresponding break on its neighboring row. The colored close-up (especially the red and white paired runs) repeats the same impression. Some displacement could be due to oblique perspective and the fact that supports partially occlude one row, so the exact offset is not measurable. The evidence supports **staggered or at least non-guaranteed break positions**, not a shared break line across both tubes.

For a printable display model, a reasonable photo-faithful convention is to offset joints in the two rows by roughly 5–15% of the straight-run length, then vary the offset slightly by leg. That range is an implementation inference from the apparent close-up displacement, not an archival dimension.

## Suggested section schedule for the topper

If the parent model needs visibly individual tube sections, use this as a constrained visual schedule:

| Region of each visible track | Starting schedule | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Long horizontal upper arm | One measured example: 4 pieces; joints near ~0.258, ~0.500, and ~0.739 of the horizontal x-span | Moderate for the photo coordinates, low for generalizing to every arm |
| Long sloping outer legs | 2–3 pieces; roughly quarter-to-two-fifths run lengths | Moderate |
| Apex-to-shoulder steep legs | 2–3 pieces; avoid a conspicuous gap at the apex | Moderate |
| Short inner-star legs | 1–2 pieces where scale leaves little room | Low to moderate |
| Immediately around any tip/notch | Short terminal pieces at each adjoining leg; maintain a lit visual connection through the corner | Moderate as a fabrication convention, low as an exact historical claim |

For each pair, keep the overall paths parallel and close, but do not assume a shared global dash phase. The measured red horizontal example has interruptions that appear approximately aligned between the paired rows, while Schumin's oblique close-ups suggest phase differences elsewhere. Supports, parallax, and tube glow make a universal “stagger every pair” rule too strong. Vary section boundaries by run, and use the measured 0.258/0.500/0.739 pattern only for the documented arm unless additional evidence is available.

## What should not be claimed

* No public source found here gives exact break coordinates, section counts per leg, tube diameters, or electrical circuit boundaries.
* The City aerial establishes the six visible white contours, not six individual tubes and not the full hidden inventory of glass sections.
* RGB images can show a different subset of energized rows; they are unsuitable for counting the all-white paths.
* Low-angle photos distort longitudinal positions. A break that appears offset between rows may include perspective, support occlusion, or parallax; use the repeated pattern only as qualitative support for staggering.
* Replica products, MakerWorld/FSX models, and decorative artwork are not geometry evidence.

## Practical conclusion

The most defensible miniature treatment is six individually modeled visible tracks grouped as three close pairs, with each long straight leg split into approximately 2–3 sections, short terminal pieces near corners, and deliberately staggered joints between the two rows of each pair. Preserve continuous light at apexes, outer tips, and notches. Treat exact joint locations as a visual approximation until a dimensioned fabrication or restoration drawing is found.
